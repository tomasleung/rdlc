# Contribution Analysis and Rate/Ratio Decomposition Methods for BI Diagnostics

## What is the exact, correct formula for rate decomposition into population/composition/within-group effects, and who are the authoritative sources?

### Takeaway
The authoritative decomposition of a difference between two rates into a "composition" (population mix) effect and a "rate" (within-group) effect was formalized by Kitagawa (1955) and is mathematically a special case of — or closely related to — direct/indirect age-standardization used by demographers and health-statistics agencies, and to the Oaxaca (1973)/Blinder (1973) decomposition used in economics; Das Gupta (1993, U.S. Census Bureau) gives the modern, order-independent, no-interaction-term generalization for any number of factors.

### Cited Findings
- Kitagawa's method, "Components of a Difference Between Two Rates," *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 50(272), 1168–1194 (1955), explains the difference between two overall rates (e.g., two crude mortality rates) in terms of (a) differences between group-specific rates and (b) differences between population composition/age structure — stratifying on categorical factors and assigning the gap to a composition component and a specific-rate component, with an optional interaction term. — [Wikipedia: Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa%E2%80%93Oaxaca%E2%80%93Blinder_decomposition)
- A symmetric (average-weighted) modern restatement of the two-factor Kitagawa split, for category weights `w` (composition shares) and category rates `r` in populations A and B: **Rate effect = Σᵢ[(w_A,i + w_B,i)/2 · (r_A,i − r_B,i)]**; **Composition effect = Σᵢ[(r_A,i + r_B,i)/2 · (w_A,i − w_B,i)]**; the two sum exactly to the crude-rate gap (rate_A − rate_B). This averaging convention removes order-dependence but is a modern tool's notation, not verified against Kitagawa's own 1955 notation. — [metricgate.com, Kitagawa Decomposition](https://metricgate.com/calculator/kitagawa-decomposition)
- Direct standardization formula: the age(or category)-standardized rate is a weighted average of group-specific rates using a fixed/external standard population's weights: **ASR = Σᵢ wᵢrᵢ / Σᵢ wᵢ**, where rᵢ = dᵢ/nᵢ (events over population at risk in stratum i) and wᵢ is the standard population's count in stratum i. The U.S. 2000 Standard Population is the reference CDC uses domestically. — [metricgate.com, Age-Standardized Rate](https://metricgate.com/docs/age-standardized-rate/); [metricgate.com, Direct vs Indirect Standardization](https://metricgate.com/blogs/direct-vs-indirect-standardization-rates/)
- Indirect standardization formula: apply a reference population's stratum-specific rates Rᵢ to the study population's own stratum counts nᵢ to get an expected count **E = Σᵢ nᵢRᵢ**; the Standardized (Mortality) Ratio = observed/expected. Indirect standardization is preferred when the study population's own stratum-specific rates are unstable (e.g., small groups), since it only requires stratum counts and a total observed count, not reliable stratum-specific rates in the study group. — [metricgate.com, Indirect Standardization](https://metricgate.com/docs/indirect-standardization); [SAGE Encyclopedia of Epidemiology, Indirect Standardization](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/epidemiology/chpt/indirect-standardization)
- The U.S. Census Bureau's Das Gupta (1993) report, *Standardization and Decomposition of Rates: A User's Manual* (Current Population Reports P23-186), gives the generalized method for any number of factors/populations, finds the additive contributions of compositional or rate factors to the difference between two overall rates, and explicitly states the method **"does not involve any interaction effects"** — i.e., it is constructed so the factor-specific components are additive and exhaustive with no leftover cross-term. — [Census.gov, P23-186 overview](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/1993/demo/p23-186.html)
- Oaxaca-Blinder (regression form, applicable to a mean-outcome gap, e.g., a rate expressed as a group mean of a binary outcome): fit a separate linear model per group with outcome on covariates X; the mean gap decomposes as **Gap = β̂_A·E(X_A) − β̂_B·E(X_B) = β̂_A·(E(X_A) − E(X_B)) + E(X_B)·(β̂_A − β̂_B)**. The first (composition/"explained") term is the share of the gap due to between-group differences in covariate levels, valued at group A's coefficients; the second ("unexplained"/structure) term is due to differing coefficients (how the same covariate translates into outcome differently by group). A threefold version adds an explicit interaction term splitting endowments, coefficients, and their interaction. — [search summary of R `oaxaca` package docs](https://rdrr.io/pkg/oaxaca/man/oaxaca.html); [Wikipedia: Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa%E2%80%93Oaxaca%E2%80%93Blinder_decomposition)
- Original citations for the economics strand: Oaxaca, R. (1973), "Male-Female Wage Differentials in Urban Labor Markets," *International Economic Review*, 14(3), 693–709; Blinder, A. S. (1973), "Wage Discrimination: Reduced Form and Structural Estimates," *Journal of Human Resources*, 8(4), 436–455. — [Wikipedia: Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa%E2%80%93Oaxaca%E2%80%93Blinder_decomposition)
- Oaxaca & Sierminska (2025), "Oaxaca-Blinder meets Kitagawa: What is the link?", *PLOS ONE*, 20(5), e0321874, establish formally that **"all Kitagawa decompositions are OB, but not all OB are Kitagawa"** — the two frameworks coincide only when the outcome is binary (i.e., a rate/proportion), the covariates are mutually-exclusive category indicators, and OB is estimated as an OLS linear-probability model. This is the citable bridge confirming Kitagawa is the demographic/rate-specific special case of the more general Oaxaca-Blinder regression framework. — [Wikipedia: Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa%E2%80%93Oaxaca%E2%80%93Blinder_decomposition)
- Extensions making the decomposition work for >2 factors/strata in an order-independent, interaction-free way: Cho and Retherford (1973), Das Gupta (1978), and Kim and Strobino (1984). — [search summary; see metricgate.com, Kitagawa Decomposition](https://metricgate.com/calculator/kitagawa-decomposition)

### Inferences
- For the foster-dashboard use case (a utilization rate = distinct fostered animals ÷ distinct animals-in-care, broken out by centre/region/species-group), the directly applicable, citable method is the **Kitagawa/Das Gupta symmetric decomposition**: split the period-over-period change in the overall rate into (1) a "within-group rate" effect — holding each centre's/species' mix share fixed, how much did the change in each subgroup's own fostering rate contribute; and (2) a "composition/mix" effect — holding each subgroup's own rate fixed, how much did the change in the share of animals-in-care that each subgroup represents contribute. This maps directly onto the BRD's Phase-1 scope (centre, region, species group) as the stratifying factors.
- Oaxaca-Blinder is mathematically the superset framework; it is only worth invoking instead of Kitagawa if a continuous (not binary/rate) outcome or regression-adjusted covariates are needed — unlikely for a Phase-1 BI diagnostic of a simple fostering rate.

### Gaps
- Kitagawa's original 1955 notation/equations were not retrievable in full text during this research pass (the Berkeley and JSTOR links returned only citation headers); the exact 1955 formula should be verified against the JASA paper or a demography textbook (e.g., Preston, Heuveline & Guillot, *Demography: Measuring and Modeling Population Processes*) before being presented as a direct quote — the symmetric formula above is a modern restatement from a non-academic calculator site, used here only as a worked illustration of the additive-decomposition logic Das Gupta/Census.gov confirms.
- Das Gupta (1993) P23-186's exact equations were not retrieved (only the Census.gov landing-page abstract); the full PDF (census.gov/library/publications/1993/demo/p23-186.html → linked PDF) should be pulled for the literal formula if the report-writer needs the precise algebraic form rather than the confirmed properties (additive, no interaction term, generalizes to N factors).

## Is the naive "Group change ÷ Total change" contribution-ratio formula sound, and when does it break down?

### Takeaway
The naive ratio is a valid, exact decomposition only when the groups are mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, and additive (group values sum to the total in both periods) — under those conditions it is algebraically identical to one version of a bridge/waterfall decomposition; it becomes unreliable or meaningless when the total's base-period value is near zero or changes sign, and it is not even well-defined when groups overlap (e.g., non-additive distinct counts) because "group change" and "total change" are no longer related by simple arithmetic.

### Cited Findings
- When a ratio's denominator (the prior-period/base value) is small, even small measurement error in that denominator "can distort calculations of fractions dramatically" — the cited mitigation in one growth-measurement paper was to screen out/exclude observations where the change in the denominator falls below a threshold. — [arXiv, "Consumption and capital growth"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01527)
- Dividing a change by a negative or sign-flipping base value produces a misleading sign (e.g., a swing from a loss to a profit can compute as "-400% growth"); the commonly cited spreadsheet fix is to take the absolute value of the base in the denominator, but this is an ad hoc workaround, not a rigorous solution, and still distorts results when the base magnitude is small. — [small-business growth-percentage guide](https://www.thesmallbusinessexpo.com/blog/how-to-calculate-percentage-of-growth/); [Excel percent-change guide](https://office.alibaba.com/officesoftware/how-do-you-calculate-percentage-increase-or-decrease-in-excel)
- Australia's official national-accounts methodology states explicitly that **"there is no one formula that can be applied in all cases"** for quarterly chain-volume contributions to growth — the correct formula depends on the index formula used (Laspeyres vs. Fisher) and the period over which the contribution is calculated; for net exports specifically, "the sign of the contribution may be reverse to that of the change in values" because net exports is a difference of two positive aggregates (exports minus imports). — [ABS, "Contributions to growth," Australian National Accounts methodology, ch.6](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/concepts-sources-methods/australian-system-national-accounts-concepts-sources-and-methods/edition-8/chapter-6-price-and-volume-measures/contributions-growth)
- The older (pre-reference-year-chaining) ABS expenditure-GDP method computed a component's contribution to growth as **(component value this period − component value last period) ÷ GDP value last period** — i.e., exactly the "group change ÷ total's prior-period base" form — and ABS states this convention is what makes contributions sum exactly to the aggregate's growth rate (in the periods before the reference year resets). This confirms the naive-looking ratio form *is* the correct official-statistics contribution formula, but critically it divides by the **prior-period total**, not by the "total change" — a different, better-behaved denominator than the raw "Total change" naively suggested in the research brief. — [ABS, 2012 National Accounts quality declaration](https://www.abs.gov.au/AUSSTATS/abs@.nsf/Lookup/5206.0Feature+Article1Jun+2012)
- A non-additive measure (most commonly a distinct/unique count) "can't be summed across dimensions with simple addition" — the standard textbook example is counting distinct customers, where a customer who appears in two categories/segments is counted once in the union total but is double-counted if you naively sum each segment's own distinct count. Power BI's own DISTINCTCOUNT documentation treats this gap (segment counts summing to more than the whole) as expected behavior, not a defect. — [AtScale, Non-Additive Measures documentation](https://documentation.atscale.com/installer/I2023.4.1/creating-and-sharing-cubes/creating-cubes/modeling-cube-measures/types-of-cube-measures/non-additive-measures); [Power BI DISTINCTCOUNT validation guide](https://www.refontelearning.com/blog/power-bi-distinctcount-total-grain-validation)
- A bridge/waterfall chart is only a valid "total = sum of parts" explanation when **"Starting value + Sum of all drivers = Ending value"** reconciles exactly; a residual exceeding roughly 10% of the total change signals the decomposition is missing real drivers (a sign the chosen factor set, or the arithmetic linking them, is incomplete/wrong) — a direct, practical check for whether a naive contribution split is trustworthy. — [search synthesis of FP&A/variance-analysis sources](https://spreadsheetcenter.com/excel-exercises/revenue-waterfall-decomposition)

### Inferences
- The brief's candidate formula "Group change ÷ Total change" (dividing by the *change* in the total, not the total's *level*) is the weakest, most fragile variant: it inherits the small-denominator and sign-flip problems described above whenever the total's change itself is near zero (e.g., a province-wide count that barely moved even though individual centres moved a lot in offsetting directions) — this is strictly worse-behaved than the official-statistics convention of dividing by the prior-period **level** of the total, which stays well away from zero far more often.
- For the foster-dashboard context specifically, "Group change ÷ Total change" is unsound as a default formula for two independent reasons that compound: (1) province-wide totals can be small or move by a few units, making the denominator unstable; and (2) if "Fostered Animals" or "Animals in Care" is a distinct count and an animal can appear in more than one centre/region within a period (e.g., transferred), the group-level changes will not even sum to the total change, so the ratio's numerator and denominator are not from the same additive universe — the formula can produce contributions that don't sum to 100%, or that have the wrong sign, independent of the zero-denominator problem.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative source was found that evaluates the exact brief-stated formula "Group change ÷ Total change" (as opposed to "Group change ÷ Total prior-period level," which is well-documented); the inference that the "change ÷ change" form is strictly more fragile than "change ÷ level" is this researcher's reasoning from the cited small-denominator/sign-flip literature, not a direct citation — flag as inference, not confirmed fact, in the final report.

## What is the more rigorous version used in BI/finance (waterfall/bridge) and in statistics/ML (Shapley-value/SHAP), and when is the simple ratio good enough vs. when is rigor needed?

### Takeaway
BI/finance's rigorous answer is a price-volume-mix (PVM) style bridge that defines each driver's effect by holding the others fixed at a stated baseline (prior-period or current-period), which only sums exactly to the total change if an explicit interaction/residual term is included or the baselines are chosen so no residual remains (e.g., the "prior-price × volume-change, current-units × price-change" convention); statistics/ML's rigorous answer is the Shapley value, which resolves baseline-choice ambiguity by averaging the marginal contribution of each factor over **every possible ordering** of the other factors, guaranteeing exact summation (local accuracy) and fairness, at combinatorial computational cost.

### Cited Findings
- Standard two-way price-volume bridge (no interaction term, exact reconciliation by construction): **Volume effect = (Actual Units − Budget Units) × Budget Price**; **Price effect = (Actual Price − Budget Price) × Actual Units**. Equivalently, in a prior/current convention: volume effect = Δunits × prior-period price, price effect = Δprice × current-period units; this pairing sums exactly to the total revenue change, with no leftover term. — [spreadsheetcenter.com, Revenue waterfall decomposition](https://spreadsheetcenter.com/excel-exercises/revenue-waterfall-decomposition); [variance-analysis skill synthesis](https://playbooks.com/skills/anthropics/knowledge-work-plugins/variance-analysis)
- An alternative budget-based convention computes Volume Effect = (Actual Volume − Budget Volume) × Budget Price and Price Effect = (Actual Price − Budget Price) × Actual Volume, with "Mix" either left as a Residual/interaction term or allocated proportionally; a three-way version (price, volume, mix) further defines Volume Effect = (Actual Volume − Budget Volume) × Budget Price × Budget Mix, each driver computed with a different combination of actual/budget baselines for the other factors. — [variance-analysis skill synthesis](https://playbooks.com/skills/anthropics/knowledge-work-plugins/variance-analysis)
- Which period's value is held fixed for which driver (actual vs. budget volume/price/mix) determines the result, and is the source of order-dependence: a blog on FP&A analysis treats a leftover cross-term ("price-volume mix") as a necessary fourth bucket if simple two-way effects are used, computed as (Δprice × Δvolume), because leaving it out means the two primary variances won't foot to (sum to) the total. — [modelmonkey.io, Sales & CRM / FP&A framework](https://modelmonkey.io/blog/analyzing-sales-fpa-framework)
- A bridge/waterfall decomposition should satisfy "Starting value + Sum of all drivers = Ending value" exactly; practitioners are advised to keep the chart to 5–8 named drivers and bucket everything smaller into "Other," and a bridge bar shows **arithmetic** contribution only — "A bridge bar labelled 'Δ gross margin' tells you the arithmetic contribution of margin change; it does not tell you why margin changed" — i.e., bridge/waterfall analysis explains the "how much," not the causal "why." — [search synthesis of FP&A/variance-analysis sources](https://spreadsheetcenter.com/excel-exercises/revenue-waterfall-decomposition); [tickerleague.com, Waterfall vs bridge](https://tickerleague.com/learn/waterfall-and-bridge-charts)
- The Shapley value (Shapley, L.S., 1953, "A Value for n-Person Games," in *Contributions to the Theory of Games*, Vol. II, Annals of Mathematics Studies No. 28, Princeton University Press, pp. 307–317) is a unique payoff-allocation rule satisfying efficiency, symmetry, and linearity axioms, resolving exactly the baseline-order-dependence problem that plagues two-way/three-way bridges by averaging each factor's marginal contribution over all possible orderings of the other factors. — [search synthesis citing Shapley 1953 original publication details](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/BF01780630)
- Lundberg & Lee (2017), "A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions" (NeurIPS 2017; SHAP), formalize this for feature attribution with the exact formula: **ϕᵢ = Σ_{S⊆F∖{i}} [|S|!(|F|−|S|−1)!/|F|!] · [f_{S∪{i}}(x_{S∪{i}}) − f_S(x_S)]** — each factor's attribution is the weighted average, over every subset S of the other factors, of the change in the outcome from adding factor i to that subset. SHAP proves this is the **unique** attribution satisfying three properties: (1) **local accuracy/efficiency** — the attributions plus a base value sum exactly to the actual outcome; (2) **missingness** — a factor that isn't present gets zero attribution; (3) **consistency/monotonicity** — if a factor's marginal contribution rises (or stays the same) everywhere, its attribution cannot fall. — [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org, full text of arXiv:1705.07874](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874)
- Computational cost of the exact Shapley/SHAP formula grows combinatorially (on the order of 2^M terms for M factors), which is why SHAP's own paper relies on approximations: **Shapley sampling values** (sample orderings/subsets instead of enumerating all of them, assuming factor independence); **Kernel SHAP** (recast as a weighted linear regression using a specific "Shapley kernel," cost O(2^M + M³), practical for small M); **Linear SHAP** (closed-form ϕᵢ = wᵢ(xᵢ − E[xᵢ]) for a linear model with independent factors — no sampling needed at all); **Max SHAP** (exact in O(M²) for a max-type aggregation function). — [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org, full text of arXiv:1705.07874](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874)
- In marketing-attribution practice, Zhao et al. (2018) apply Shapley values to split credit for conversions across advertising channels and propose a reformulation that improves computational efficiency to make exact-ish Shapley attribution tractable at a more realistic channel count; a secondary source notes that with 20 channels the number of orderings/permutations reaches 1,048,576, making the brute-force formula impractical without such reformulations or sampling. — [search synthesis; Zhao et al. 2018 channel attribution](https://lib.hutech.edu.vn/chi-tiet?id=161884)

### Inferences
- The "simple ratio is good enough" regime is: groups are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, the measure is additive (SUM-based, not DISTINCTCOUNT across overlapping entities), and the total's prior-period level is comfortably away from zero — in that regime, a bridge/PVM-style decomposition with explicitly stated baselines (and, ideally, an interaction term so it reconciles exactly) is both sufficient and far more defensible to a business stakeholder than SHAP, because it is transparent and auditable in a single DAX measure or spreadsheet formula.
- Shapley/SHAP-style rigor becomes necessary specifically when (a) there is no natural "baseline" ordering to assign interaction effects to (more than 2-3 drivers, or drivers interact strongly), or (b) the business needs a provably fair/unique allocation (e.g., for audit or incentive-compensation purposes) rather than just "a reasonable-looking bridge." For a Phase-1 foster-utilization dashboard with 3 simple diagnostic factors (centre/region, species mix, and within-group rate), a two/three-way bridge is almost certainly sufficient; full SHAP would be over-engineering unless stakeholders specifically contest the fairness of a two-way split.

### Gaps
- No source was found giving a named, citable "spreadsheet-ready" worked example of SHAP/Shapley applied to a *generic* BI KPI change (as opposed to ad-tech channel attribution or portfolio performance attribution); the generalization to an arbitrary BI contribution problem is this researcher's inference from the general SHAP formula, not a directly cited worked example.

## Is there a simplified, spreadsheet/DAX-implementable version of Shapley-value contribution a BI team could realistically apply, and what is lost by simplifying?

### Takeaway
Yes — for a small, fixed number of drivers (2–4, which covers a typical centre/region/species-group breakdown), the **Shapley value can be computed exactly in closed form by enumerating and averaging over all driver orderings**, which is a finite, DAX/Excel-tractable sum (not simulation); what's lost going from full SHAP (designed for many ML features) to this simplified BI version is nothing in exactness for small driver counts, but the approach does not scale past roughly 5–6 drivers without falling back to sampling/approximation, and it still requires the analyst to pre-define what counts as a "driver" (e.g., rate vs. mix vs. volume), which SHAP itself does not do automatically.

### Cited Findings
- The exact Shapley/SHAP formula is a finite sum over all subsets S of the other M−1 factors, weighted by |S|!(M−|S|−1)!/M! — for M=2 or M=3 factors (e.g., "rate effect" and "mix effect," or "price," "volume," "mix") this is only 2 or 6 orderings respectively, fully enumerable by hand or in a handful of DAX measures; cost only becomes exponential/intractable as M grows (the paper's own examples cite 2^M scaling and, in channel-attribution use, up to 20 factors yielding over 1 million orderings). — [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org, full text of arXiv:1705.07874](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874); [search synthesis on channel-count scaling](https://lib.hutech.edu.vn/chi-tiet?id=161884)
- For exactly 2 drivers (e.g., a rate decomposition into "within-group rate effect" and "composition/mix effect"), averaging the two possible orderings' marginal contributions produces precisely the **symmetric Kitagawa formula already cited above** [Rate effect = Σ(w_avg · Δr), Composition effect = Σ(r_avg · Δw)] — i.e., for the two-factor case, the "simplified Shapley" and the "Kitagawa/Das Gupta additive decomposition" are the same arithmetic object. This is this researcher's own derivation from the two cited formula families (Shapley averaging-over-orderings, and the symmetric Kitagawa restatement), not a separately sourced claim, but it follows directly from both formulas' structure. — [metricgate.com, Kitagawa Decomposition](https://metricgate.com/calculator/kitagawa-decomposition); [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org SHAP formula](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874)
- Linear SHAP gives a fully closed-form, no-enumeration-needed special case for a linear/additive model with independent factors: **ϕᵢ = wᵢ(xᵢ − E[xᵢ])** — directly translatable to a single DAX measure per driver if the underlying relationship between drivers and the KPI is linear and the drivers don't interact. — [ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org, full text of arXiv:1705.07874](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874)
- Das Gupta's standardization/decomposition method (U.S. Census Bureau, P23-186) is explicitly engineered to have **no interaction effects** and to generalize additively to any number of factors — functioning, in effect, as the BI/demography field's own pre-built "simplified Shapley for rates," arrived at independently of game theory decades earlier. — [Census.gov, P23-186 overview](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/1993/demo/p23-186.html)

### Inferences
- A practical DAX-implementable recipe for a BI team follows directly from the above: (1) identify 2-3 drivers of the rate change (e.g., within-centre rate change, centre-mix shift); (2) for each driver, compute its "effect" using the period-averaged weight/rate convention (the symmetric Kitagawa form); (3) verify the effects sum exactly to the observed total rate change (a Das-Gupta-style no-interaction-term guarantee) as the audit check. This gives a Shapley-equivalent, order-independent, fully-reconciling result without ever invoking Shapley/SHAP terminology or tooling — the business audience only needs to see "rate effect" + "mix effect" = total change.
- What's genuinely lost relative to full SHAP: (a) SHAP's missingness and consistency properties are proven for arbitrary M-factor games with arbitrary (even non-linear, non-additive) underlying functions; the simplified 2-3-driver spreadsheet version only inherits these guarantees when the true relationship between drivers and KPI is itself additive/bilinear (true for rate = Σ(share×rate) decompositions, not necessarily true for arbitrarily complex KPI formulas); (b) scaling past ~5 drivers requires either accepting non-exact sampling or collapsing drivers into fewer buckets, which SHAP in ML contexts handles routinely via Monte Carlo but a spreadsheet/DAX team would need bespoke tooling to replicate.

### Gaps
- No source directly documents a named "spreadsheet Shapley" or "DAX Shapley" product/pattern; the above recipe is this researcher's synthesis connecting the cited SHAP formula structure to the cited Kitagawa/Das Gupta formula structure, flagged for the report-writer to present as a reasoned bridge between the two literatures rather than as an independently-sourced established BI technique.

## How does non-additivity (an entity counted in multiple subgroups in the same period) break naive contribution-ratio formulas, and what is the standard mitigation?

### Takeaway
A distinct/unique count is non-additive by definition — the union-level total is less than or equal to the sum of per-segment counts whenever any entity belongs to more than one segment in the period — so both "group change ÷ total change" and most two-way bridge formulas silently assume an additivity that doesn't hold, producing contributions that don't sum to the total (or to 100%) and can even carry the wrong sign; the standard mitigation is to pick an explicit allocation rule (methods exist specifically for this, e.g. incremental/Shapley-style contribution allocation) rather than naively summing or ratio-ing raw per-segment distinct counts.

### Cited Findings
- Non-additive measures "can't be summed across dimensions with simple addition," and distinct/unique counts are the textbook example: counting distinct customers who bought a product is non-additive because a customer who buys more than once, or who falls into two categories, is counted once in a combined total but could be counted twice if each category's count is summed separately. — [AtScale, Non-Additive Measures documentation](https://documentation.atscale.com/installer/I2023.4.1/creating-and-sharing-cubes/creating-cubes/modeling-cube-measures/types-of-cube-measures/non-additive-measures)
- A Power BI-specific validation walkthrough confirms the exact mechanism relevant to this project: a customer (or, in this project's domain, an animal) who appears in two categories is counted once in the union/overall total but twice if you sum each category's own DISTINCTCOUNT; Microsoft's own DISTINCTCOUNT documentation treats the resulting mismatch (overall total being less than the sum of parts) as expected, correct behavior, not a bug to fix. — [refontelearning.com, Power BI DISTINCTCOUNT total-grain validation](https://www.refontelearning.com/blog/power-bi-distinctcount-total-grain-validation)
- Atoti's documentation names this specific problem "contribution analysis" for non-additive measures and gives examples such as allocating firm-wide economic capital down to constituent trading desks; it explicitly states that the top-level value of a distinct-item count will not match the sum of its contributors when broken down by a dimension like store or city, and frames the fix as needing an **explicit allocation rule** (it discusses "incremental contribution" as one such rule) rather than naive summation. — [search synthesis of Atoti's non-additive-measures article series](https://www.atoti.io/articles/how-to-explain-non-additive-measures-part-2-incremental-contribution/)

### Inferences
- For the foster-dashboard case specifically (animals in care / fostered, broken out by centre/region), if any animal can be associated with more than one centre within the same reporting period (e.g., an inter-centre transfer, or a multi-location intake-to-foster pipeline), then neither "sum of each centre's distinct-count change" nor "each centre's change ÷ province total's change" will reconcile to the province-level change; this is a structural (not a calculation) error, so the standard mitigation is one of: (a) **define the grain so each animal is attributed to exactly one centre per period** (e.g., centre-of-record on a specific snapshot date) before computing any contribution split, which restores additivity and makes the simple ratio/bridge formulas valid again; or (b) if multi-centre membership in a period is itself meaningful and must be kept, apply an explicit allocation/incremental-contribution rule (conceptually the same "hold other segments' membership fixed, measure the marginal addition of each segment" logic that underlies both PVM bridges and Shapley values) rather than a naive ratio.
- Given the project's `CLAUDE.md` "Known Gaps" section does not currently flag entity-level multi-centre/multi-period overlap as a modeling risk, and the mock data is a controlled, seeded, referentially-intact 600-row fixture, this specific failure mode may or may not be present in the Phase-1 Foster model's actual fact grain — that should be checked against the confirmed Table Definitions document (`02-table-definitions/TABLE_DEFINITIONS_FOSTER_v1_3.docx`) before this finding is treated as applicable, rather than assumed.

### Gaps
- No source gives a single named, canonical "standard mitigation" formula for the overlapping-distinct-count case (as opposed to the general principle of "define an explicit allocation rule" or "fix the grain to be mutually exclusive"); a deeper, dedicated search of OLAP/semantic-modeling literature on "non-additive measure allocation" or "distinct count attribution" would be needed for a more specific, formally-citable algorithm beyond the principle-level guidance found here.
