# Sensitivity Analysis & Seasonal Comparison Methods for Governed BI Diagnostics

## Key Question 1: Is "jackknife resampling" a legitimate citable grounding for the BI practice of "does this finding survive excluding the top contributor / an anomalous period," or is that a loose analogy? What is the BI practice actually called in governance literature?

### Takeaway
The formal statistical jackknife (Quenouille 1949, named/extended by Tukey) is a specific procedure for estimating the *bias and standard error of an estimator* by systematically recomputing it with one observation left out at a time and aggregating the leave-one-out values with a defined scaling formula. The BI practice of re-running a diagnostic finding with the top contributor or an anomalous period excluded is **structurally similar (leave-one-out recomputation) but not formally equivalent** — it is missing the statistical aggregation step (no bias/SE estimate is computed from the leave-one-out values), and in BI it is almost always applied as a single deliberate exclusion of a *known, suspect* unit rather than a systematic exhaustive loop over all units to characterize an estimator's sampling properties. The precise, non-overclaiming term for the BI practice is "leave-one-out sensitivity check" or, more broadly, "robustness check" / "robustness analysis" — not "jackknife" unless the full leave-every-observation-out-in-turn procedure with variance/bias aggregation is actually being run.

### Cited Findings
- The jackknife estimator is formed by leaving out each observation from a dataset in turn, computing the estimate on the remainder, and aggregating those leave-one-out values — this aggregation (and a specific standard-error formula) is the defining feature, not merely "exclude an observation and recompute." — [Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)
- The jackknife standard error is the scaled standard deviation of the leave-one-out estimates, with scaling factor (T−1)/T; this is a specific, named formula, not an informal "check if it still looks right." — [Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)
- Quenouille introduced the method in 1949 to reduce bias in an estimator; Tukey later recognized the same leave-one-out values could also yield a standard error, and he coined the name "jackknife" because it is "a rough-and-ready tool that can improvise a solution for a variety of problems." — [Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)
- The jackknife is known to fail (not degrade gracefully, but be formally invalid) for statistics like the median and other quantiles, because small perturbations to the data don't change them smoothly — a concrete case where the formal method breaks down even for professional use. — [Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)
- The jackknife is deterministic (exhaustive leave-one-out loop over all n observations); the bootstrap draws random resamples with replacement and has largely superseded the jackknife in general statistical practice. — [Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)
- In applied/BI-adjacent practice, "leave-one-out" (LOO) is used generically to mean "re-estimate a model or result with one observation (or group, or study) removed to see whether a single unit drives the result" — this is the loose, BI-compatible usage, distinct from the formal jackknife's full aggregation procedure. — [scispace.com LOO explainer](https://scispace.com/resources/sensitivity-you-can-explain-leave-one-outs-and-model-variants/); [arxiv.org/pdf/2512.09652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.09652)
- A Python panel-data diagnostics library (PanelBox) treats "sensitivity to subsamples" and "influence diagnostics" as a named robustness-checklist category alongside outlier detection and alternative specifications, explicitly stating the goal as "results should not depend on a handful of observations, a single time period, or a particular set of assumptions" — this is close to the standard umbrella term for the BI practice. — [panelbox.readthedocs.io](https://panelbox.readthedocs.io/en/latest/diagnostics/robustness/)
- PanelBox operationalizes a concrete, exportable threshold rule: flag a unit as influential/fragile if dropping it moves a coefficient by more than a set number of standard errors, or if dropping it flips statistical significance. — [panelbox.readthedocs.io/.../sensitivity](https://panelbox.readthedocs.io/en/latest/diagnostics/robustness/sensitivity/)
- Cook's distance is the standard formal measure for flagging influential/high-leverage observations in regression, with a common rule-of-thumb cutoff of 4/n — this is the rigorous statistical cousin of "does one record drive the result," but it requires a regression model, which most BI diagnostic findings (a simple rate or trend comparison) do not have. — [r-statistics.co](https://r-statistics.co/Sensitivity-Analysis-in-R.html)
- A 2025 conference paper on robustness/sensitivity frameworks states plainly that "there is no agreed way to conduct and report robustness checks," and that practice across quantitative research is inconsistent and hard to replicate — i.e., even in academic/formal settings there is no single named, standardized checklist framework that the BI practice could claim direct lineage from. — [isaconf.confex.com Paper162576](https://isaconf.confex.com/isaconf/forum2025/webprogram/Paper162576.html)
- A management-research literature review (robustness analysis in quantitative management research) defines robustness analysis as testing whether findings hold across alternative, defensible analytical choices, and separately finds that robustness reporting in leading management journals is itself narrow, inconsistent, and scattered across sections — reinforcing that "robustness check"/"robustness analysis" is the correct umbrella academic term, but even formal literature lacks a single canonical checklist. — [research.nottingham.edu.cn robustness analysis review](https://research.nottingham.edu.cn/en/publications/robustness-analysis-in-quantitative-management-research-a-review-/)
- Warning against reverse-engineering legitimacy: one source explicitly warns against dropping outliers merely to improve a result's significance/appearance ("never remove outliers just to improve p-values") — directly relevant caution for a BI diagnostic-governance skill, since the exclusion must be pre-specified/principled (e.g., a defined low-volume threshold or a flagged extraordinary period), not chosen after seeing that it changes the conclusion favorably. — [r-statistics.co](https://r-statistics.co/Sensitivity-Analysis-in-R.html)

### Inferences
- The precise, defensible language for a BI governance skill is: call the practice "robustness check" or "sensitivity check" (generic, legitimate, widely used across econometrics, management research, and applied data science) and explicitly avoid calling it "jackknife resampling" unless the skill actually implements the exhaustive leave-every-record-out loop with bias/variance aggregation — which is unlikely to be the typical BI diagnostic use case (typically only 1–3 deliberate exclusions are tested: the top contributor, a known anomalous period, a low-volume segment).
- "Leave-one-out" (LOO) is the safer, more literally accurate borrowed term when a BI skill excludes one specific unit (e.g., the top contributor) and recomputes — it names the mechanic without claiming the formal statistical procedure's guarantees (bias correction, standard-error estimate).
- Because no single canonical named framework exists even in the academic robustness-analysis literature, a BI governance skill should not claim to be "following [Named Framework]" — instead it should describe its own checklist transparently (which exclusions were tested, why they were chosen a priori) and cite the general robustness-analysis literature as precedent for the *practice*, not for a specific named protocol.

### Gaps
- No official statistics agency, audit standard (e.g., AICPA, ISA), or BI governance body (e.g., DAMA DMBOK, ISO 8000) was found to have a named, standard checklist specifically for "diagnostic finding robustness" in the way the user described (exclude low-volume groups / exclude anomalous periods / re-run against prior snapshot / vary baseline) as a single codified framework. The closest formal analogues found are: (a) general "robustness analysis" in econometrics/management research (inconsistent, no single checklist), (b) influence diagnostics (Cook's distance) in regression, and (c) real-time/vintage data analysis in macroeconomics (see Key Question 2 area) for the "re-run against a prior snapshot" piece specifically. A targeted search of DAMA DMBOK or ISO 8000 parts 60s/100s (data governance process reference model) text itself (behind paywalls/previews in search results) could not be directly fetched to confirm or rule out more specific terminology.

## Key Question 2: What do official statistics agencies recommend as standard caveats/practice for comparing a current period against historical benchmarks, particularly around sample-size/volume thresholds below which a comparison shouldn't be reported or should be flagged low-confidence?

### Takeaway
Official statistics and public-health agencies converge on a consistent, citable pattern: (1) set a hard minimum count/cell-size threshold below which a rate is not published or not compared at all (commonly 3–10 depending on agency and context), (2) flag rates computed from counts in an intermediate "unstable" band (commonly cited as roughly 3–19 or similar) with an explicit reliability warning symbol rather than suppressing them outright, and (3) separately check whether the observed value represents a real departure from an established trend before treating a period-over-period or vs-benchmark difference as meaningful. There is no single universal numeric threshold — each agency sets its own — but the *pattern* (hard floor + flagged intermediate band + trend-context check) is consistent and directly portable to a BI governance skill.

### Cited Findings
- UK ONS policy: "It is ONS policy not to calculate mortality rates where there are fewer than three deaths in a cell, on grounds of statistical quality," because "rates based on such low numbers are susceptible to random fluctuation from year to year and inaccurate interpretation." — [ONS FOI response, infant mortality rates](https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/infantmortalityrates)
- ONS flags an intermediate "low but not suppressed" band: "rates that are based on between 3 and 19 deaths are displayed in tables but are denoted (u) as a warning to the user" about reliability — a concrete, citable two-tier threshold model (hard floor at 3, soft/flagged band 3–19). — [ONS FOI response](https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/infantmortalityrates)
- A US state vital-statistics methodology note states a rate based on 20 cases has a 95% confidence interval roughly as wide as the rate itself, and recommends pooling several years of data where single-period counts are too small — a specific, quantified rationale (not just a rule of thumb) for why small-count comparisons should be flagged or aggregated. — [Oregon vital statistics methodology](https://www.oregon.gov:443/oha/PH/BIRTHDEATHCERTIFICATES/VITALSTATISTICS/ANNUALREPORTS/VOLUME2/Documents/2006/stepbyst.pdf)
- Arkansas Department of Health standard: suppress all non-zero counts below 10, and also suppress rates derived from those counts, using secondary/complementary suppression so a hidden cell can't be back-calculated by subtraction from published totals — a concrete disclosure-control technique relevant if a BI tool similarly rolls up/derives totals from small sub-cells. — [ADH Small Numbers Standards, March 2025](https://healthy.arkansas.gov/wp-content/uploads/ADH-Small-Numbers-Standards-Final-March.2025.pdf)
- General statistical-disclosure guidance: "many statisticians consider a cell size of 3 to be the absolute minimum needed to prevent disclosure," with larger minimums (5 or 10) used for stronger protection — note this rationale is about *privacy/disclosure risk*, not statistical reliability per se, but agencies often apply the same numeric floor for both reasons simultaneously. — [BTS/BEA FCSM disclosure methodology survey](https://www.bts.gov/sites/bts.dot.gov/files/docs/learn-about-bts-and-our-work/statistical-methods-and-policies/202321/200309.pdf)
- For business-data (not population) statistics, suppression is often driven by *dominance rules* rather than raw counts: a Statistics Canada-style rule suppresses a cell if a single contributor accounts for more than 80% of the cell total; BLS's "pq-rule" suppresses when the largest contributors' combined share of the total exceeds a threshold set by two parameters (p, q). This is the more relevant model for BI diagnostics about centres/contributors (e.g., "is this centre's result dominated by one or two cases?") rather than population counts. — [FCSM 2003 Provencal paper](https://apps.bea.gov/icsp/fcsm/assets/docs/2003FCSM_Provencal.pdf); [BLS research paper on pq-rule](https://www.bls.gov/osmr/research-papers/2007/pdf/st070080.pdf)
- A UK Office for Statistics Regulation (OSR) compliance-review letter notes that NHS waiting-times statistics now "routinely include a caution" in monthly releases, and that a Public Health Scotland impact-assessment process exists specifically to help users distinguish a change caused by a methodology/guidance change from a genuine underlying change — a direct precedent for the practice of flagging "this change may be an artifact of a process/definition change, not a real trend," which maps onto the user's "re-run against a prior snapshot to check for restatement effects." — [OSR correspondence, waiting-times compliance review](https://osr.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/correspondence/scott-heald-to-siobhan-tuohy-smith-compliance-review-of-inpatient-day-case-and-outpatient-stage-of-waiting-times-statistics-follow-up/)
- A statistics-literacy guide ("How to Spot Spin") recommends explicitly asking "is the latest figure a departure from the previous trend?" before treating a single-period comparison as meaningful, and separately warns that comparisons across long periods can be spurious if many other factors changed in between. — [bookdown.org "How To Spot Spin"](https://bookdown.org/profrobwells/bookdown-demo/Documents/How_To_Spot_Spin.pdf)
- US BLS Current Employment Statistics (CES) methodology: cut-off sample sizes/types were not derived from a formula in isolation but were "empirically derived over time" by checking which sample designs gave results that tracked well against independent administrative benchmark sources — i.e., official statistics agencies validate a sampling/comparison approach against an external benchmark rather than relying purely on a priori formulas. — [BLS, "100 Years of CES Sample Design"](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2016/article/one-hundred-years-of-current-employment-statistics-sample-design.htm)
- Eurostat publishes an explicit, numeric table of minimum sample-size reliability guidelines for EU Labour Force Survey annual estimates, broken out by country and year, and these published thresholds changed between 2008 and 2014 — demonstrating that even official minimum-reliability thresholds are reviewed and revised periodically, not fixed forever. — [Eurostat LFS reliability limits metadata](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/Annexes/hrst_esms_an_6.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible, portable rule set for a BI governance skill, synthesized from the above precedents: (a) define a hard minimum-volume floor below which a comparison is not shown at all (agencies commonly use 3–10 for population counts; a dominance-style rule, e.g. "no single record/contributor >50–80% of the cell," is more appropriate when the "population" is a small number of centres/contributors rather than individual case counts); (b) define an intermediate band where the comparison is shown but visibly flagged low-confidence (ONS's 3–19 pattern is a workable template — e.g., "fewer than N transactions: interpret with caution"); (c) separately validate that an observed change is a departure from trend, not just a point-in-time blip, before calling it a finding at all.
- The "re-run against a prior snapshot to check for restatement/data-correction effects" piece of the user's practice (A) does not have a single official-statistics name as a sample-size rule, but is well precedented by the OSR/NHS "distinguish a methodology-change artifact from a real change" caveat, and more rigorously by the macroeconomic concept of "real-time / vintage data" analysis (see Key Question 1 area and macro literature: Croushore & Stark 2003 is the classic reference establishing that published findings should be checked across data vintages because revisions can overturn them). This is the most directly citable formal grounding for that specific sub-practice.

### Gaps
- Did not find an official statistics agency source giving a single universal recommended minimum-volume threshold applicable across contexts (it is agency- and context-specific by design, since it depends on the underlying variance of the measure). A BI governance skill should set its own threshold and document the rationale rather than citing a single borrowed number as "the" standard.
- Did not find a named framework specifically combining all four BI practices (exclude low-volume / exclude anomalous periods / re-run against prior snapshot / vary baseline) under one official label in audit or BI-governance literature; the AICPA/ISA audit-sampling angle requested in the brief was not directly surfaced in search results within the tool budget and should be treated as a gap rather than asserted.

## Key Question 3: What's the standard, citable guidance on choosing a 5-year vs 3-year vs other-length seasonal baseline window, and on how to handle a known structural break inside that window?

### Takeaway
There is no single universal "use N years" rule; guidance frames it as a tradeoff (more years = more stable seasonal factor estimates but higher risk of spanning a regime change; fewer years = more responsive but noisier), and the most concretely citable real-world precedent for *handling a known structural break inside the window* is the UK ONS excess-mortality methodology, which explicitly and permanently excluded the pandemic-disrupted year (2020) from its 5-year rolling baseline average rather than including it, with a stated rationale and documented successive window choices.
- Note: the Hyndman & Athanasopoulos *Forecasting: Principles and Practice* (OTexts, free online, fpp3/fpp2) textbook chapter on STL could not be retrieved with its exact baseline-length recommendation text in this research pass — its STL chapter content below (on strengths/limits of STL itself) was retrieved, but it does not appear to prescribe a specific "N years of history" rule for a seasonal baseline comparison; that is more a seasonal-adjustment-agency practice (X-13ARIMA-SEATS/X-11 tradition) than an STL-textbook rule.

### Cited Findings
- A seasonal-adjustment methodology study (presented to a Philippine statistics forum) recommends using roughly 5 to 15 years of quarterly or monthly data for X-11-style seasonal adjustment, explicitly to accommodate possible changes in the underlying structure of the series, and tests this guidance via simulations covering (a) no structural change, (b) an abrupt level shift, and (c) gradual change — but the paper itself describes its own numbers as "rules of thumb," not a hard standard. — [Philippine Statistical Association study, tps_2011_60_1_3](https://mail.psai.ph/docs/publications/tps/tps_2011_60_1_3.pdf)
- EViews' X-13 seasonal-adjustment implementation enforces a minimum of 3 full years of data as a technical floor, and supports up to 600 observations (50 years monthly / 150 years quarterly) — this is a software/technical constraint, not an official substantive recommendation, but establishes 3 years as a commonly used practical floor. — [EViews X-13 documentation](https://help.eviews.com/content/seriescmd-x13.html)
- A method for detecting a structural break specifically in the *seasonal profile itself* (not just the trend level) uses a Chow F-test applied to the 12-month seasonal profile across candidate break years; in a US housing-market application, F-statistics were small/insignificant for every candidate year from 2014–2020 and rose sharply at 2021, cleanly identifying the break year — this is a concrete, replicable statistical test a more sophisticated analyst could run to check whether a chosen lookback window is contaminated by a break, rather than just assuming it. — [arXiv "From Summer to Spring: A Shift in US Housing Market Seasonality"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.21358)
- A paper on seasonal fractional integration finds that seasonal patterns in US macro series "have changed over time," meaning a long averaging window can blend together genuinely different seasonal regimes rather than one stable pattern — direct support for the risk side of "longer window = more stable but may average over a real regime change." — [arXiv/related work on deterministic vs stochastic seasonal fractional integration and structural breaks](https://repository.londonmet.ac.uk/488/)
- **ONS excess-mortality baseline — concrete precedent for handling a known structural break inside the window:** "It was always the case with our previous approach that data for 2020 were removed when calculating the five-year average number of deaths" — the pandemic-disrupted year was excluded from the baseline by design, every year, specifically because "including 2020 would greatly increase the number of deaths we would class as 'expected' each week, which would reduce the measured excess." — [ONS FOI response FOI/2023/4804](https://cy.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/excessdeathsmethodologyandtheinclusionofpandemicyears); [ONS blog, "Understanding excess deaths during a pandemic"](https://blog.ons.gov.uk/2022/01/12/understanding-excess-deaths-during-a-pandemic)
- The ONS window itself rolled forward year by year while keeping the exclusion: for the 2022 calculation the baseline years were 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021 (2020 skipped); for 2023 it was 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022 (2020 still skipped) — a documented, repeatable pattern of "keep a fixed N-year window length, but surgically drop the known-anomalous year(s) and substitute the next valid year," rather than either (a) including the anomaly or (b) shortening the window. — [EAP196, "Calculating the excess mortality baseline," UK Statistics Authority](https://uksa.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/EAP196_Calculating-_the_excess_mortality_baseline.pdf)
- NISRA (Northern Ireland Statistics and Research Agency) reports that a new UK-wide excess-deaths methodology introduced in February 2024 still publishes the 5-year averages both with and without 2020 side-by-side for comparison/transparency — i.e., official practice is to show the structural-break-sensitivity check itself (both versions) to the reader, rather than silently picking one. — [NISRA excess deaths methodology note](https://nisra.gov.uk/node/3910)
- Criticism of the ONS count-based approach: outside commentators noted that a raw-count 5-year-average baseline ignores population size and age-structure changes, and that using age-standardised mortality rates (ASMRs) instead of raw counts makes the measured "excess" smaller — a caution that a baseline-average method can be distorted not just by a one-off anomalous year, but by slow structural denominator changes (population growth/ageing) that a simple average doesn't adjust for. — [Institute and Faculty of Actuaries blog, "Measures of excess deaths"](https://blog.actuaries.org.uk/2023/2/measures-of-excess-deaths/)

### Inferences
- The ONS precedent generalizes directly to the user's BI scenario: when a known structural break/anomalous period (e.g., a one-time centre closure, a shelter-policy change, a data-system migration) falls inside the lookback window, the defensible pattern is to (1) keep the window length constant but exclude the anomalous period(s) and substitute the next-oldest valid period to preserve N years of comparison data, and (2) disclose both the "as calculated" and "including the anomaly" versions side-by-side when feasible, rather than silently choosing one.
- There is no authoritative single number ("always use 5, never 3") — the tradeoff is real and the choice should be justified in a governance skill as: longer window → smoother/more stable seasonal baseline, more likely to span a break (requiring the exclude-and-substitute treatment above); shorter window → more current/responsive but noisier and more exposed to single-year idiosyncrasies. 3 years is a commonly used practical/technical floor; 5 years is a commonly used default in both X-11-style agency practice and the ONS mortality precedent; longer (5–15 years) is sometimes recommended specifically to make the seasonal *factor estimate itself* more stable, which is a related but distinct goal from choosing a comparison baseline window.
- Formally testing for a break in the seasonal profile (Chow-test-on-seasonal-profile method) is a legitimate, citable statistical technique, but it is squarely in "requires more statistical sophistication than a business analyst in DAX/Power BI would typically deploy" territory — for the BI skill, the realistic substitute is a judgment-based flag ("do you, the data owner, know of an operational change inside this window?") rather than an automated statistical break-detection test.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the exact Hyndman & Athanasopoulos fpp2/fpp3 text on recommended baseline window length (the STL chapter fetched addresses STL's mechanical strengths/limits, not multi-year baseline-window choice — this is likely because fpp's focus is forecasting/decomposition method choice, not "how many years of history to use as a comparison baseline," which is more an official-statistics seasonal-adjustment-practice question than a textbook-decomposition-theory question). A follow-up read of the fpp3 chapters on "Time series decomposition" overview and "Seasonal adjustment" (rather than the STL-specific subsection) might surface more on this if needed.
- Did not find retail-sector-specific (vs. official-statistics/macro-sector) guidance on 3- vs 5-year baseline choice; the closest is the general X-11/X-13 seasonal-adjustment literature above, which is agency/macro-indicator context, not retail/operational-KPI context. This should be flagged as an extrapolation if used in the final reference.

## Key Question 4: Is there recognized best practice for disclosing "this finding was tested for robustness against X, Y, Z and held / did not hold" in a business-facing report, as opposed to a pure academic robustness-check writeup?

### Takeaway
No single named standard was found for business-facing robustness disclosure specifically, but the available guidance converges on a clear, actionable principle: state plainly which checks were run and whether the finding survived each one, in specific plain-language terms — and avoid both extremes of (a) hiding a caveat/limitation and (b) burying a stable finding under so many generic hedges that it reads as a failure. Academic robustness-reporting itself is criticized as inconsistent even in its home literature, so a business report that is more disciplined and transparent about "checked X, held" / "checked Y, did not hold" is already ahead of common academic practice, not behind it.

### Cited Findings
- A review of robustness-analysis practice in management research found that robustness reporting even in "leading management journals is often narrow, inconsistent, and scattered across sections" — meaning there is no single canonical disclosure template to borrow even from the closest academic analogue. — [Robustness analysis in quantitative management research: a review](https://research.nottingham.edu.cn/en/publications/robustness-analysis-in-quantitative-management-research-a-review-/)
- A 2025 conference paper states there is "no agreed way to conduct and report robustness checks," with practice described as inconsistent and hard to replicate across quantitative research generally — reinforcing that a business report cannot cite one authoritative academic template, but must design its own disclosure convention. — [isaconf.confex.com Paper162576](https://isaconf.confex.com/isaconf/forum2025/webprogram/Paper162576.html)
- Guidance on communicating AI-assisted/analytical work recommends stating context, methodology, and caveats openly as a defense against being perceived as having selectively reported only favorable results. — [arXiv 2505.01643, third-party compliance reviews for frontier AI safety frameworks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01643)
- The same communication-practice guidance warns that over-hedging is itself a failure mode: "piling on caveats can make a working result sound like a failure," and argues "under-claiming is as misleading as over-claiming" — i.e., disclosure should be calibrated and specific, not maximally cautious by default. — [claudecertificationguide.com, "Communicating value & limitations"](https://claudecertificationguide.com/ccao-f/learn/4-workflow-integration/4-5-communicating-value-limitations)
- The same source argues that disclosing a caught error or a check that failed can *strengthen* trust in the overall report, because hiding it makes readers doubt the rest of the work — directly supporting "held / did not hold" disclosure for checks that genuinely changed the picture, rather than only reporting checks that confirmed the headline finding. — [claudecertificationguide.com](https://claudecertificationguide.com/ccao-f/learn/4-workflow-integration/4-5-communicating-value-limitations)
- A corporate-reporting example (Vodafone, cited in a CR Dialogues paper) found that hedged assurance language like "limited assurance" can backfire by making readers think *less* rigorous work was done than actually occurred — a caution that vague or jargon-heavy caveat language in a business report can under-communicate the rigor actually applied, arguing for concrete, specific disclosure ("tested against the top-3 contributors; conclusion unchanged") over vague hedging ("results have some limitations"). — [3blmedia.com, "Mind the Gaps"](https://app.3blmedia.com/news/mind-gaps)
- The OSR/NHS precedent (also cited under Key Question 2) of "routinely including a caution" in monthly statistical releases, specifically to help readers distinguish a measurement/methodology artifact from a genuine change, is a concrete, real-world example of a governed, business/public-facing (non-academic) disclosure practice already operating at a national-statistics level — a usable template: a short, standard, routinely-included caveat sentence attached to the finding itself. — [OSR correspondence](https://osr.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/correspondence/scott-heald-to-siobhan-tuohy-smith-compliance-review-of-inpatient-day-case-and-outpatient-stage-of-waiting-times-statistics-follow-up/)

### Inferences
- For a governed BI diagnostic skill, the actionable disclosure convention synthesized from the above is: attach a short, standardized, always-present line to each diagnostic finding naming (a) which specific robustness checks were run (e.g., "excl. top contributor," "excl. [named anomalous period]," "re-run vs. prior snapshot," "alternate baseline window"), and (b) a one-word/short-phrase result per check ("held" / "weakened" / "reversed"), rather than either a blanket disclaimer or no disclosure at all. This mirrors the NHS/OSR "routine caution" model and avoids both the over-hedging failure mode and the selective-reporting failure mode identified above.
- Because even academic robustness-reporting is acknowledged to be inconsistent, a BI governance skill's main defensible claim is methodological transparency and consistency of its own convention (always disclosing the same categories of checks, every time, pass or fail) — not alignment to an external canonical standard, since none was found to exist.

### Gaps
- Did not find audit-standard-specific (ISA/AICPA/PCAOB) guidance on disclosing analytical-procedure robustness checks to a business audience within the tool budget available; this was a named "suggested source" angle in the brief (audit-sampling guidance) that could not be substantiated and should be treated as an open gap rather than inferred.
- Did not find a named framework (e.g., a "Robustness Disclosure Standard") in BI/analytics vendor documentation (e.g., Power BI, Tableau governance guides) — the disclosure practice above is synthesized from adjacent literatures (management-research methodology criticism, AI-communication guidance, UK national-statistics correspondence), not from a BI-specific source, and should be presented as a synthesized best practice rather than a citation to one named standard.

## Key Question 5 (Objective B): Compare and contrast YoY, multi-year seasonal average, and STL/classical decomposition as seasonal baseline methods

### Takeaway
The three methods trade off simplicity/interpretability against robustness to noise and sophistication required: YoY is simplest and most business-legible but conflates trend, base-year anomalies, and genuine seasonal effects into one number and reports with a built-in lag relative to the true current trend; a multi-year (e.g., 5-year) average baseline smooths out single-year noise and is still business-legible, but is vulnerable to being skewed by a structural break or an outlier year sitting inside the averaging window unless that year is deliberately handled (per Key Question 3's ONS precedent); STL/classical decomposition produces the statistically "cleanest" separation of trend, seasonal, and remainder components and is robust to occasional unusual observations, but requires real statistical literacy to interpret correctly (e.g., knowing to read the trend-cycle component, not the seasonally-adjusted series, for turning points) and is the hardest of the three to explain to and defend before a non-technical business stakeholder.

### Cited Findings
- **YoY — strength (seasonality):** YoY comparison is widely described as "naturally normalizing for seasonality" because it compares the same calendar period a year apart. — [Grain/vendor blog on seasonal trends](https://www.grainql.com/blog/seasonal-trends-date-range-analysis)
- **YoY — weakness (lag):** A U.S. Census Bureau working paper on seasonal-adjustment alternatives identifies the central, formally derived limitation of using year-over-year change as a seasonal-adjustment substitute: it is inherently delayed relative to a proper month-to-month (seasonally adjusted) comparison, giving "an outdated story" of the current trend. — [Census Bureau working paper rrs2012-05](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2012/adrm/rrs2012-05.pdf) (Note: this paper's full text could not be extracted as readable text in this research pass — the finding above is from the earlier WebSearch summary of this source, not a direct-quote fetch, and should be verified against the primary PDF before being cited as a direct quotation.)
- **YoY — weakness (base-year distortion):** An unusually high or low base year can hide real underlying progress or create a false impression of change; one practitioner source recommends using multi-year averages as the base, or comparing against industry benchmarks, specifically to counteract this. — [endai.com, "The Importance of Comparative Data"](https://www.endai.com/blog/the-importance-of-comparative-data-aka-comp-data/)
- **YoY — weakness (structural breaks / regime change):** YoY comparisons mislead when the underlying product/market/operations changed materially within the comparison year (a cited example: a pandemic, a competitor launch, or a policy change) — the prior year's figure becomes a poor baseline, not because of seasonality, but because of a one-off regime shift. — [aspireapp.com, "Year over year in finance explained"](https://aspireapp.com/blog/year-over-year-in-finance-explained)
- **YoY — weakness (trend/seasonal conflation, directly on point):** A reporting-methods guide explicitly notes that a business with steady real growth can "appear stagnant if comparisons are made between periods of high and low seasonal activity," and recommends detrending methods such as seasonal decomposition specifically to separate the trend from the seasonal effect — this is the clearest direct statement in the sources found that YoY-style period comparisons can conflate trend and seasonal signal, and that decomposition is the named remedy. — [Grain/vendor blog on seasonal trends](https://www.grainql.com/blog/seasonal-trends-date-range-analysis)
- **Multi-year average baseline — mechanism and strength:** The ONS's excess-mortality baseline methodology is the clearest real-world example: a rolling 5-year average of counts for the same calendar period, used specifically because a single prior year is too noisy/idiosyncratic to serve as "the" expected value. — [UK Statistics Authority EAP196](https://uksa.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/EAP196_Calculating-_the_excess_mortality_baseline.pdf)
- **Multi-year average baseline — weakness (structural break inside the window) and the standard remedy:** As detailed under Key Question 3, the ONS had to explicitly exclude the pandemic-disrupted year (2020) from its 5-year window every year going forward, because leaving it in "would greatly increase the number of deaths we would class as 'expected,'" which would mechanically shrink the measured excess — a textbook demonstration of a single anomalous year inside an averaging window distorting the baseline unless surgically excluded. — [ONS FOI response FOI/2023/4804](https://cy.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/excessdeathsmethodologyandtheinclusionofpandemicyears)
- **Multi-year average baseline — weakness (ignores slow structural change, not just one-off breaks):** Commentators noted the ONS's raw-count average baseline does not adjust for population size or age-structure change over the window, and that using age-standardized rates instead measurably shrinks the computed "excess" — showing the averaging-window method can be distorted by gradual denominator drift, not just a single dramatic anomalous year. — [Institute and Faculty of Actuaries blog](https://blog.actuaries.org.uk/2023/2/measures-of-excess-deaths/)
- **STL — strengths:** STL (unlike X-11/SEATS) can handle any type of seasonality (not just monthly/quarterly), allows the seasonal component to change over time at a user-controlled rate, allows user-controlled trend-cycle smoothness, and has a "robust" fitting option that keeps occasional unusual observations from distorting the trend-cycle and seasonal estimates (their effect is absorbed into the remainder component instead). — [Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, fpp3, STL chapter](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html)
- **STL — limitations:** STL produces only additive decompositions (a multiplicative pattern must be approximated via log transform or a Box-Cox transform with 0<λ<1), and it does not automatically handle trading-day/calendar variation (e.g., different numbers of weekdays per month) the way some other methods do. — [fpp3 STL chapter](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html)
- **STL/seasonally-adjusted series — interpretation hazard directly relevant to "requires more statistical sophistication":** The textbook explicitly warns that a seasonally adjusted series still contains the remainder/noise component, so it is not smooth, and short-term "downturns" or "upturns" visible in the seasonally adjusted series alone can be misleading; to correctly identify turning points, the analyst should look at the trend-cycle component specifically, not the seasonally adjusted series — a nuance a business analyst unfamiliar with decomposition terminology could easily get wrong (e.g., presenting a noisy seasonally-adjusted blip as a real turning point). — [fpp3 STL chapter](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html)
- **STL — authoritative textbook citation for the reference table:** Hyndman, R.J. & Athanasopoulos, G., *Forecasting: Principles and Practice*, OTexts, Melbourne — free, open-access, and widely cited; 3rd edition (2021) and 2nd edition (2018) both exist online. — [OTexts fpp3](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html); [general citation confirmation via search](https://biblioteca.confindustria.it/search/detail/forecasting-principles-and-practice/71469)

### Inferences
- For the "achievable with DAX/Power BI vs needs more sophistication" framing requested in the brief: YoY and a multi-year average baseline are both directly achievable with simple recompute-and-compare DAX measures (a YoY measure, or an N-year rolling average measure, are both standard, well-documented DAX patterns); STL decomposition is materially harder to implement correctly in DAX/Power BI natively (it is an iterative Loess-smoothing procedure) and, even if implemented (e.g., via an R/Python visual or a pre-computed external pipeline), requires the report author to correctly explain the trend-cycle-vs-seasonally-adjusted distinction to avoid stakeholders misreading noise as a turning point — this should be flagged in the final reference table as the clearest "needs more statistical sophistication than a typical business analyst workflow" item of the three methods.
- The single clearest, most quotable "what each one hides" line for the eventual reference table: YoY hides/conflates trend changes and base-year anomalies behind a seasonality-normalized-looking number and reports late; a multi-year average hides/gets distorted by any structural break or gradual structural drift sitting inside its own averaging window unless that break is deliberately and transparently excluded; STL's seasonally-adjusted output still contains noise that can look like a trend turn to an untrained reader, who must be told to read the trend-cycle component instead.
