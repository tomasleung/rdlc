# Eight diagnostic methods, ranked by feasibility

The candidate list of eight diagnostic techniques is directionally sound, but each one needs to be split into a DAX-buildable "Must" version and a specialist "Avoid" version rather than treated as a single technique — and the real risk in a self-service BI diagnostic skill is not picking the wrong formula but misusing an otherwise-correct one: dividing a contribution by a denominator that can cross zero, summing a non-additive distinct count across overlapping segments, importing antitrust concentration bands into an operational context, running a global outlier rule on a seasonal series, or treating a statistical signal as proof of a business cause. **Contribution/rate decomposition** has a single authoritative, order-independent answer — the Kitagawa (1955) / Das Gupta (1993, U.S. Census Bureau) additive split into a within-group "rate effect" and a population "mix effect" — which for two or three drivers is mathematically identical to a Shapley-value attribution, so a BI team gets Shapley's fairness guarantee without ever touching Shapley/SHAP tooling ([Census.gov, P23-186](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/1993/demo/p23-186.html); [Wikipedia, Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa%E2%80%93Oaxaca%E2%80%93Blinder_decomposition)). **Mix-adjustment/standardization** inherits a clean, decades-old epidemiological rule for which variant to use: direct standardization when every group's category-specific rate is reliable, indirect (expected-vs-actual, SMR-style) when cells are sparse ([CDC/NCHS, Age adjustment](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/hus/sources-definitions/age-adjustment.htm); [Health Knowledge, Standardisation](https://www.healthknowledge.org.uk/e-learning/epidemiology/specialists/standardisation)). **Concentration analysis** splits into three different tools for three different questions — top-N/Pareto share for a one-sentence stakeholder headline, HHI for an internal concentration *trend*, and Gini/Lorenz for the full shape of imbalance — and HHI's legal antitrust thresholds must never be quoted as a business verdict ([ABS, Contributions to growth](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/concepts-sources-methods/australian-system-national-accounts-concepts-sources-and-methods/edition-8/chapter-6-price-and-volume-measures/contributions-growth); [Paul Weiss, 2023 Merger Guidelines](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/doj-and-ftc-issue-final-2023-merger-guidelines)). **Change-point and outlier detection** both reduce, for self-service BI, to the same DAX-buildable primitive — a seasonal-baseline standardized residual (same-calendar-period, multi-year mean or median) read against simple control-chart rules — while CUSUM, PELT, and Bayesian change-point detection stay out of scope because they need recursive state, whole-series optimization, or posterior computation DAX cannot natively express ([Western Electric/Nelson rules via QualityGurus](https://www.qualitygurus.com/nelson-rules-and-western-electric-rules-for-control-charts/); [Killick, Fearnhead & Eckley 2012 via JSS](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v058i03)). **Sensitivity analysis** should be renamed "leave-one-out robustness check," not "jackknife," because the formal jackknife requires an exhaustive leave-every-observation-out loop with a bias/variance aggregation step that BI diagnostics never perform ([Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)). **Seasonal comparison** has a clear simplicity/robustness ladder — year-over-year, then multi-year seasonal average with explicit anomaly exclusion, then STL decomposition — where only the first two are self-service feasible ([Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, fpp3, STL chapter](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html)).

## Kitagawa decomposition settles the contribution-ratio question

The research brief's candidate "contribution analysis/decomposition" and "rate/ratio decomposition" are not two techniques — they are the same statistical family, and that family has a named, authoritative answer. Kitagawa's 1955 paper, "Components of a Difference Between Two Rates" (*JASA* 50(272)), splits the gap between two overall rates into a **composition effect** (driven by differences in group population shares) and a **rate effect** (driven by differences in within-group rates), and Das Gupta's 1993 U.S. Census Bureau manual (P23-186) generalizes this to any number of factors in a form that is explicitly additive with **no interaction term** ([Census.gov, P23-186](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/1993/demo/p23-186.html)). A modern symmetric restatement — Rate effect = Σᵢ[(w_A,i + w_B,i)/2 · (r_A,i − r_B,i)], Composition effect = Σᵢ[(r_A,i + r_B,i)/2 · (w_A,i − w_B,i)] — sums exactly to the observed change in the overall rate, which is the auditability property a business stakeholder needs to trust the split ([metricgate.com, Kitagawa Decomposition](https://metricgate.com/calculator/kitagawa-decomposition)). Crucially, this averaged-weights formula is not an ad hoc BI shortcut: for exactly two drivers it is mathematically identical to averaging the marginal contribution of each factor over every possible ordering of the other — which is precisely the definition of a **Shapley value** (Shapley, 1953), the unique attribution rule that satisfies efficiency, symmetry, and linearity and that modern ML explainability (SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017) re-derives as the only attribution with guaranteed local accuracy ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org, SHAP paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874)). A 2025 paper by Oaxaca & Sierminska makes the family relationship formal and citable: "all Kitagawa decompositions are OB [Oaxaca-Blinder], but not all OB are Kitagawa," with the two coinciding exactly when the outcome is a rate and the covariates are mutually exclusive category indicators ([Wikipedia, Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitagawa%E2%80%93Oaxaca%E2%80%93Blinder_decomposition)). The practical upshot for a BI skill: a stakeholder can be shown "rate effect + mix effect = total change" without the words "Shapley" or "SHAP" ever appearing, because for two or three diagnostic drivers (centre/region, species mix, within-group rate) the exact Shapley computation collapses to a handful of enumerable orderings — fully tractable in a few DAX measures — whereas full SHAP is designed for many-feature ML models and only becomes necessary past roughly five or six interacting drivers, where enumeration turns combinatorial (2^M terms; marketing-attribution literature cites over one million orderings at 20 channels) ([search synthesis, channel-attribution scaling](https://lib.hutech.edu.vn/chi-tiet?id=161884)).

The naive "group change ÷ total change" formula that motivated the brief's candidate list is the weakest member of this family, and it fails for two independent, compounding reasons relevant to a fostering-rate dashboard. First, dividing by the *change* in the total (rather than the total's prior-period *level*) inherits a severe small-denominator instability: official national-accounts practice (Australia's ABS) instead divides a component's change by the total's prior-period **level**, specifically because that denominator stays further from zero, and ABS states plainly that "there is no one formula that can be applied in all cases" for contribution-to-growth calculations — the correct form depends on the index convention and can even flip sign when the aggregate is itself a difference of two positive quantities ([ABS, Contributions to growth](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/concepts-sources-methods/australian-system-national-accounts-concepts-sources-and-methods/edition-8/chapter-6-price-and-volume-measures/contributions-growth)). Second, and more specific to this project's data shape, a **distinct count is not additive**: if an animal can appear in more than one centre's "animals in care" count within the same reporting period (a transfer, for example), the centre-level distinct-count changes will not sum to the province-level change at all — Power BI's own DISTINCTCOUNT documentation treats a union total being smaller than the sum of its parts as expected, correct behavior, not a bug ([refontelearning.com, Power BI DISTINCTCOUNT total-grain validation](https://www.refontelearning.com/blog/power-bi-distinctcount-total-grain-validation)). The standard mitigation is not a cleverer ratio but a modeling fix: either define the grain so each animal has exactly one centre-of-record per period (restoring additivity so the simple bridge formulas work again), or, if genuine multi-centre membership must be preserved, apply an explicit allocation rule rather than a naive sum. The more rigorous BI/finance alternative to the naive ratio — a price-volume-mix (PVM) bridge, e.g., Volume effect = (Actual − Budget units) × Budget price, Price effect = (Actual − Budget price) × Actual units — reconciles exactly only when an interaction/residual term is included or the baselines are chosen deliberately; practitioners recommend capping a bridge at five to eight named drivers and treating any residual over roughly 10% of total change as a sign the driver set is incomplete, and explicitly warn that "a bridge bar tells you the arithmetic contribution... it does not tell you why" — a bridge is a decomposition, never a causal explanation ([search synthesis, FP&A/variance-analysis sources](https://spreadsheetcenter.com/excel-exercises/revenue-waterfall-decomposition)).

## Indirect standardization rescues sparse centre-level rates

Mix-adjustment/standardization is the field's answer to a specific confound that a simple centre-by-centre rate comparison cannot handle: two centres can have genuinely different utilization rates for every intake type yet look different overall purely because they serve different intake-type mixes. CDC/NCHS formalizes **direct standardization** as a weighted average of each group's own category-specific rates, using a single shared "standard population" mix as weights — Age-adjusted rate = Σ[r_i × (p_i/P)] — which makes every group directly comparable on one common scale, but only if a reliable category-specific rate exists for every group ([CDC/NCHS, Age adjustment](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/hus/sources-definitions/age-adjustment.htm)). **Indirect standardization** flips the data requirement: it applies one trusted reference rate per category to each group's own actual mix counts to get an expected count, then reports the ratio of observed to expected (the Standardized Mortality/Morbidity Ratio, or SMR) — Expected = Σ(n_i × r_i), SMR = Observed/Expected — and needs only each group's mix counts plus one shared reference rate set, not a reliable rate from every group ([Wikipedia, Standardized mortality ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_mortality_ratio); [StatsDirect, Indirect Standardization and SMR](https://statsdirect.com/help/rates/smr.htm)). The decision rule that both authoritative sources converge on is unambiguous and quotable to a non-technical stakeholder: "indirect standardisation is more appropriate for use in studies with small numbers or when the rates are unstable" ([Health Knowledge, Standardisation](https://www.healthknowledge.org.uk/e-learning/epidemiology/specialists/standardisation)). Translated to a 32-centre foster network, that means: if every centre has enough volume in every intake-type cell to trust a centre-level, type-specific rate, use direct standardization and get a genuinely comparable "as-if-typical-mix" rate per centre; if some centres are small or some intake-type cells are thin, use indirect standardization instead and present the result as an actual-vs-expected index, with the explicit caveat that SMR-style ratios from different centres are not strictly comparable to each other because each one implicitly weights by its own mix — only direct standardization gives one truly shared comparison scale ([StatsDirect, Indirect Standardization and SMR](https://statsdirect.com/help/rates/smr.htm)). Neither variant was found to carry a sourced minimum-cell-size threshold specific to this choice; that threshold should be set and documented by the BI team itself rather than borrowed from an unrelated domain, mirroring the small-cell suppression practice described below.

## Top-N share beats HHI for stakeholder headlines

"Concentration analysis" is really three distinct questions wearing one label, and matching the right tool to the right question matters more than computing all three. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) — the sum of squared group shares, ranging 0–10,000 in percentage-point terms — condenses an entire share distribution into one trend-trackable number, but its conventional interpretation bands are a legal artifact, not a general statistical fact: the 2023 DOJ/FTC Merger Guidelines define "highly concentrated" as a post-merger HHI above 1,800 with a presumption of antitrust harm at a >100-point increase, a tightening from the 2010 Guidelines' 2,500/1,500 bands — thresholds calibrated specifically to a legal test of competitive harm in a defined market, not to any general notion of "too concentrated" ([Paul Weiss, 2023 Merger Guidelines](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/doj-and-ftc-issue-final-2023-merger-guidelines)). Importing those numeric bands into an operational question like "is foster placement concentrated among a few centres" borrows a regulatory verdict that was never calibrated for that purpose; HHI is legitimately usable outside antitrust (trade concentration, banking deposit concentration) but only as a *relative*, trend-over-time diagnostic — this quarter's HHI versus last quarter's, for the same network — never as a stand-alone "concentrated/not concentrated" verdict against the DOJ bands ([ABS methodology discussion context](https://www.abs.gov.au/AUSSTATS/abs@.nsf/Lookup/5206.0Feature+Article1Jun+2012)). HHI also mechanically falls as the number of groups rises even with no change in evenness (its minimum is 1/n), so comparing HHI across networks or time periods with different group counts is not apples-to-apples without correcting for n.

**Top-N share / cumulative Pareto curve** — "the top 5 of 32 centres handle 61% of all placements," built as a sort-descending, running-total-percentage DAX measure — is the method that actually answers a stakeholder's literal question in one sentence and renders as a self-explanatory chart, and practitioner guidance explicitly treats it as the complement to, not a substitute for, HHI ([Umbrex, Product Portfolio Concentration](https://umbrex.com/resources/company-analysis/product-management/product-portfolio-concentration/); [Power BI community, Cumulative for Contribution of Sales](https://community.fabric.microsoft.com/t5/Desktop/Cumulative-for-Contribution-of-Sales/m-p/1457897)). The **Lorenz curve/Gini coefficient** — G = A/(A+B), the area between the equality line and the Lorenz curve over the area under it, computable in a spreadsheet as the mean absolute pairwise difference between group values divided by twice n² times the mean — is the right tool specifically when the audience needs to see the *shape* of imbalance across every group, not just the top few versus the rest, but it has two caveats that argue for keeping it an analyst-facing visual rather than a stakeholder headline: a single Gini number cannot show *where* the inequality sits (two very differently-shaped distributions can share one Gini value, and Lorenz curves that cross cannot be validly ranked by Gini alone), and a peer-reviewed finding (Deltas, 2003, *Review of Economics and Statistics*) shows Gini is **downward-biased with few units** — directly relevant to a 32-centre network, which is a small group count by the standard this bias literature uses ([MetricGate, Lorenz Curve](https://metricgate.com/calculator/lorenz-curve); [IDEAS/RePEc, Deltas 2003](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v85y2003i1p226-234.html)).

## Seasonal baselines, not global IQR, catch real outliers

Change-point detection and outlier/anomaly detection are listed as separate techniques in the brief, but for a self-service BI skill they collapse into the same buildable primitive and diverge only at the specialist tier. The core, DAX-feasible move for *both* is the same: remove the seasonal pattern before testing anything for being unusual. A **seasonal-baseline standardized residual** — deviation = observed period minus the mean (or, more robustly, median) of the same calendar period across the prior four to five years, standardized by that same-period spread — is the exact methodology official excess-mortality surveillance uses in production (UK: 5-year same-period mean; Germany: 4 years; Belgium: 11), and it is fully buildable as a DAX measure with a rolling same-month lookback ([arXiv, "Excess deaths, baselines, Z-scores..."](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.10320); [EuroMOMO, What is a z-score?](https://euromomo.eu/how-it-works/what-is-a-z-score)). Applying a **global IQR fence** directly to a raw seasonal series instead — the naive alternative — misfires in a specific, predictable way: Tukey's fence (Q1 − 1.5×IQR / Q3 + 1.5×IQR) is calibrated to flag roughly 0.7% of values under an IID, non-seasonal, roughly-normal distribution, a property a seasonal series does not have, so a global fence will systematically flag every normal peak-season month as an "outlier" year after year while remaining under-sensitive to a genuine anomaly hiding in a normally-quiet month ([arXiv, "Unifying Boxplots"](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20259v1); [Splunk, Time Series Decomposition](https://docs.splunk.com/Documentation/DSP/1.0.1/FunctionReference/TimeSeriesDecomposition)). For the baseline's own spread calculation, MAD (median ± k×1.4826×MAD) should replace standard deviation whenever the lookback history is short or might itself contain anomalous prior years, because SD has a 0% breakdown point — even one extreme historical value inflates it and masks future real anomalies — while median-based MAD tolerates up to 50% contamination before breaking down; a demonstrated example shows a single injected outlier moved an SD-based ratio to ~1.46 but a MAD-based ratio to only ~1.03 ([MathWorks, mad function documentation](https://www.mathworks.com/help/stats/mad.html)). Once the seasonal-baseline residual exists, simple, well-documented **SPC decision rules** (Western Electric, 1956: one point beyond 3σ, or 8 consecutive points on one side of the baseline) make a defensible, DAX-buildable "flag for investigation" trigger ([QualityGurus, Nelson Rules](https://www.qualitygurus.com/nelson-rules-and-western-electric-rules-for-control-charts/)).

Everything past this point needs tiering carefully. **CUSUM**'s formula (two running sums, each floored at zero, flagged against a threshold h) is mathematically a running-total-with-floor, which a DAX analyst familiar with running-total patterns could in principle build, but no published DAX recipe for it was found, and the real burden is choosing defensible parameters (target mean, sensitivity k, threshold h) for seasonal count data — a raw monthly count fed straight into CUSUM would trigger false "shifts" purely from a seasonal swing unless deseasonalized first ([CUSUM, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CUSUM)). **PELT** (Killick, Fearnhead & Eckley, 2012, *JASA*) is a fundamentally different kind of operation: an offline, whole-series optimization that finds the best way to cut an entire history into segments by minimizing a penalized cost function — it has no DAX analog at all, and only exists in R (`changepoint`) or Python (`ruptures`), requiring a local installation and, in the Power BI Service, viewer-side R/Python availability ([JSS, changepoint R package](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v058i03); [Microsoft Learn, R-powered custom visuals](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-in/power-bi/create-reports/desktop-r-powered-custom-visuals)). **Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection** returns a full posterior probability rather than a binary flag and is squarely specialist territory ([Knogin, Bayesian changepoint detection](https://knogin.com/en/developers/bayesian-changepoint-detection)). The **Chow test** is the one exception worth a middle tier: it is genuinely spreadsheet-feasible (three OLS regressions via Excel's LINEST or the Analysis ToolPak, F-statistic against F.INV.RT), but only when an analyst already has a specific, nominated candidate break date in mind ("did the rate shift after the March 2023 policy change?") — it cannot discover an unknown break date, which would require the more specialist sup-F test ([MetricGate, Chow Test methodology](https://metricgate.com/calculator/chow-test)). Across every one of these methods, the official-statistics and SPC literatures converge on the same guardrail, worth hard-coding as standard caveat language on any flagged result: a rule violation "suggests an assignable cause — it does not prove one exists," and a statistical outlier is explicitly *not* the same thing as a data error — both are possibilities to investigate, never default conclusions ([QualityGurus, Nelson Rules](https://www.qualitygurus.com/nelson-rules-and-western-electric-rules-for-control-charts/); [US Census Bureau, Coleman 2002](https://census.gov/library/working-papers/2002/econ/2002-coleman.html)).

## Leave-one-out checks outrank jackknife, and YoY outranks STL

The brief's "sensitivity analysis" candidate is best grounded not in the formal statistical jackknife but in a cousin practice, and naming it correctly matters for a governed skill's credibility. The jackknife (Quenouille, 1949; named by Tukey) is a specific procedure: leave out each observation in turn, recompute the estimate, and aggregate the leave-one-out values via a defined bias/standard-error formula — a BI team excluding one known top contributor or one anomalous period and recomputing is structurally similar (leave-one-out recomputation) but is missing that aggregation step, and is a single deliberate exclusion rather than an exhaustive loop ([Jackknife resampling, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknife_resampling)). The precise, non-overclaiming term is **"leave-one-out robustness check,"** the same umbrella term used in panel-data diagnostics literature, which operationalizes a concrete exportable rule: flag a result as fragile if dropping a unit moves the conclusion by more than a set threshold or flips its direction ([panelbox.readthedocs.io](https://panelbox.readthedocs.io/en/latest/diagnostics/robustness/)). One explicit discipline matters here: exclusions must be pre-specified (a known top contributor, a flagged anomalous period) and never chosen *after* seeing that they favorably change the conclusion — "never remove outliers just to improve the result" ([r-statistics.co](https://r-statistics.co/Sensitivity-Analysis-in-R.html)). No single official-statistics or audit standard was found codifying this exact BI checklist (exclude top contributor / exclude anomalous period / re-run against prior snapshot / vary baseline) under one name, so a governed skill should describe its own checklist transparently rather than claim alignment to a named external framework — and should disclose which checks were run and whether each one held or reversed, since even academic robustness reporting is criticized as inconsistent, meaning a business report with a short, routinely-included "tested against X, held" sentence is already ahead of common academic practice ([research review, robustness analysis in management research](https://research.nottingham.edu.cn/en/publications/robustness-analysis-in-quantitative-management-research-a-review-/)). A separate, well-grounded piece of the same practice is the volume-threshold question: official statistics agencies converge on a hard floor below which a comparison should not be shown at all (UK ONS: no mortality rate below 3 events in a cell) plus an intermediate band shown but visibly flagged (ONS: 3–19 events marked "(u)" for reliability), and for business/contributor-style data a dominance rule (no single contributor above roughly 50–80% of a cell) is the more relevant template than a raw population-count floor ([ONS FOI response](https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/infantmortalityrates); [FCSM/BLS pq-rule](https://www.bls.gov/osmr/research-papers/2007/pdf/st070080.pdf)).

For seasonal comparison, the three methods form a clean simplicity-versus-robustness ladder, and only the first two clear the self-service bar. **Year-over-year** comparison is business-legible and "naturally normalizes for seasonality," but it conflates trend, base-year anomalies, and seasonal effect into a single number and formally lags the true current trend — a U.S. Census Bureau working paper identifies this lag as the central limitation of using YoY as a seasonal-adjustment substitute ([Census Bureau working paper rrs2012-05](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2012/adrm/rrs2012-05.pdf)). A **multi-year seasonal-average baseline** (commonly 5 years) smooths that single-base-year noise and stays business-legible, but it is vulnerable to a structural break sitting inside the averaging window unless that break is deliberately handled — the clearest real-world precedent is UK ONS's excess-mortality methodology, which permanently excludes the pandemic-disrupted year 2020 from its rolling 5-year baseline, substituting the next valid year to keep the window length constant, and which publishes both the "with" and "without" versions side by side for transparency rather than silently choosing one ([UK Statistics Authority, EAP196](https://uksa.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/EAP196_Calculating-_the_excess_mortality_baseline.pdf); [NISRA excess deaths methodology](https://nisra.gov.uk/node/3910)). **STL decomposition** (Cleveland et al., 1990) produces the statistically cleanest trend/seasonal/remainder separation and its robust-fitting option resists distortion from occasional unusual observations, but it is an iterative Loess-smoothing procedure with no DAX equivalent, and even when implemented via an external R/Python step it carries a specific misread risk the textbook itself flags: the seasonally-adjusted series still contains noise, so a short-term blip in it can look like a real turning point to an untrained reader, who must be told to read the trend-cycle component instead ([Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, fpp3](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html)).

| "Is our network becoming more or less concentrated over time?" | HHI (sum of squared shares) | **Prefer**, analyst-facing trend only | A single index sensitive to both group count and evenness, trackable period over period ([DOJ, HHI calculation](https://www.justice.gov/atr/appendix-1)) | Mechanically falls as group count rises even with no evenness change; antitrust bands (1,800/100-pt) are a legal threshold, not a business one | Presenting HHI's absolute antitrust bands as a stakeholder verdict on an unrelated operational question |
| "What's the full shape of imbalance across all centres?" | Lorenz curve / Gini coefficient | **Prefer**, with caution | Visualizes/scores inequality across the entire distribution, not just top vs. rest ([MetricGate, Lorenz Curve](https://metricgate.com/calculator/lorenz-curve)) | Downward-biased with few groups (Deltas, 2003); one Gini value can hide very different distribution shapes | Few groups (e.g., ~32 centres) without disclosing the small-sample bias; as a sole stakeholder headline (too abstract) |
| "Did this period/centre fall outside its normal seasonal pattern?" | Seasonal-baseline standardized residual (same-period multi-year mean/median ± SD or MAD) + SPC rules (Western Electric/Nelson) | **Must** | Flags a period as deviating from its own seasonal expectation, using a well-documented control-chart decision rule ([QualityGurus, Nelson Rules](https://www.qualitygurus.com/nelson-rules-and-western-electric-rules-for-control-charts/); [arXiv, excess-mortality baselines](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.10320)) | A flag is "investigate," never "confirmed cause or error" ([US Census Bureau, Coleman 2002](https://census.gov/library/working-papers/2002/econ/2002-coleman.html)); use MAD over SD when the baseline history is short or itself may contain anomalies | Applying a single global threshold across a strongly seasonal raw series without first removing the seasonal pattern |
| Same question, naive version | Global IQR fence on raw (non-deseasonalized) series | **Avoid** | Nothing useful on seasonal data — it is calibrated for IID, non-seasonal distributions | Tukey's 1.5×IQR fence flags ~0.7% of values only under non-seasonal, roughly-normal data ([arXiv, Unifying Boxplots](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20259v1)) | Any series with a recurring seasonal peak/trough — it will flag every normal peak month and miss real off-season anomalies |
| "Did the metric's trend level genuinely and sustainedly shift?" | Seasonally-adjusted CUSUM, Bayesian changepoint detection (BOCPD), PELT | **Avoid** (self-service) | Formally detects sustained mean shifts (CUSUM), full posterior changepoint probability (BOCPD), or optimal whole-series segmentation (PELT) ([CUSUM, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CUSUM); [JSS, PELT](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v058i03)) | No native DAX recursion/whole-series optimization; needs R/Python and parameter tuning (k, h, penalty) | Any case where a seasonal-baseline SPC flag already serves the "worth investigating" purpose |
| "Did the metric shift after this specific known event/date?" | Chow test (pre-specified break date) | **Prefer**, only with an analyst-nominated date | Tests whether a regression relationship differs before/after one hypothesized break date, spreadsheet-feasible via LINEST/ToolPak ([MetricGate, Chow Test](https://metricgate.com/calculator/chow-test)) | Requires a *known* candidate date; cannot discover an unknown break date | The break date is unknown — needs a sup-F test or PELT, both specialist territory |
| "Does this finding survive dropping the top contributor or a weird period?" | Leave-one-out robustness check (not "jackknife") | **Must** | Tests whether a single unit is driving an otherwise business-relevant conclusion ([panelbox.readthedocs.io](https://panelbox.readthedocs.io/en/latest/diagnostics/robustness/)) | Exclusions must be pre-specified (a priori), never chosen after seeing they help the conclusion ([r-statistics.co](https://r-statistics.co/Sensitivity-Analysis-in-R.html)) | Using it to justify discarding inconvenient data after the fact; claiming it as the formal statistical jackknife |
| "Is this comparison reliable given how few records/animals it's based on?" | Minimum-volume / dominance floor with flagged intermediate band | **Must** | States plainly whether a cell has enough volume to trust, following the ONS hard-floor-plus-flag pattern ([ONS FOI response](https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/infantmortalityrates)) | No universal threshold exists — the BI team must set and document its own floor and dominance rule | Comparing or ranking cells below the documented floor as if they were equally reliable |
| "How does this period compare to a 'normal' season?" | Year-over-year comparison | **Must** | Same-calendar-period comparison, simplest and most business-legible | Lags the true current trend and conflates trend with base-year anomaly ([Census Bureau, rrs2012-05](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2012/adrm/rrs2012-05.pdf)) | The prior-year base itself was anomalous (policy change, closure) — use a multi-year average instead |
| Same question, more robust | Multi-year seasonal-average baseline (4–5 yr), with known-anomaly exclusion | **Must**/**Prefer** | Smooths single-year noise into a more stable "expected" seasonal value ([UK Statistics Authority, EAP196](https://uksa.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/EAP196_Calculating-_the_excess_mortality_baseline.pdf)) | A structural break inside the window must be surgically excluded and disclosed, not silently averaged in | A known anomalous period sits inside the window and has not been excluded/disclosed |
| Same question, highest rigor | STL / classical seasonal-trend decomposition | **Avoid** (self-service) | Statistically cleanest trend/seasonal/remainder split, robust to occasional unusual points ([Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, fpp3](https://otexts.com/fpp3/stl.html)) | Iterative Loess fitting, no DAX equivalent; the seasonally-adjusted output still contains noise that can be misread as a turning point | A business stakeholder will read the seasonally-adjusted series directly rather than the trend-cycle component |

## Conclusion

The research converges on one structural insight the original eight-item list did not surface on its own: nearly every "advanced" technique in this space (CUSUM, PELT, BOCPD, STL, Shapley/SHAP) has a DAX-feasible sibling that recovers most of its practical value by doing one specific, cheap thing first — removing seasonality before testing for anomalies, holding a mix fixed before attributing a rate change, fixing the grain before summing a distinct count — and the sibling, not the original, belongs in a self-service skill's "Must" tier. The second takeaway is that correctness failures in this domain are rarely about the formula itself; they are about silent scope violations: a global IQR fence applied to seasonal data, an antitrust HHI band quoted as an operational verdict, a distinct count summed as if it were additive, or a statistical flag presented as a confirmed cause. A governed BI-diagnostic skill earns more trust by hard-coding the caveat sentence next to each method ("this is a signal to investigate, not a confirmed cause") than by adding more sophisticated math — the literature on both SPC and official statistics treats that discipline, not the choice of estimator, as the real safeguard.
