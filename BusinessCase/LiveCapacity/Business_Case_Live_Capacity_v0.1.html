1. The real problem: a dashboard request is not a data-product request

The request, exactly as it arrived:

"Business intent: Provide a real-time, centralized view of available space across the organization so we (Animal Flow) can make faster, more informed placement decisions. A live dashboard would consolidate capacity data into one accessible location, reducing manual follow-ups and improving operational efficiency.

Current process: space is determined by checking a report on SB and then calling/emailing CACs to confirm availability and resources as that information is not recorded on SB — i.e. staffing levels, resources, etc. This current process is time-consuming and not accurate.

Data needed: ShelterBuddy shelter locations for all shelters in the province; an Excel sheet CAC managers would fill out twice a day; Parm's physical capacity dashboard; anything else needed to see CAC-specific, region-specific, and organization-wide space." — Kahlee Demers, Senior Manager, Animal Triage and Flow, Animal Care Services

Taken literally, this reads as a request to put an existing ShelterBuddy report on a screen. What actually blocked that: ShelterBuddy's location data wasn't consistent enough to trust. Every centre named rooms its own way. Kennel was an optional field, so most centres only ever recorded the room, not the specific space. There was no way to record a space as on hold or unavailable, or why. And one centre's own location list mixed real rooms with vet hospitals, an animal-control van, an office, and Foster — all in the same dropdown.

In the product team's own words, reviewing that data before building anything:

"The data was there, but it meant something different at every centre. A dashboard on top of this data would have shown confident numbers that were wrong."

A report

ShelterBuddy's existing capacity export, pointed at a screen — confident-looking numbers nobody had actually standardized the meaning of first.

A data product

Standardized room and kennel names, an agreed status model (Open/In use/Hold/Unavailable, each with a reason), RACI, and a daily confirmation step — the governed layer that makes the number trustworthy.

2. This is human-in-the-loop AI, not agentic AI

In the product team's own words, stated twice in the external presentation of this work: "Each assistant drafts. I review and decide." and "AI assists. People decide." An agentic AI pursues a goal on its own, choosing its own steps with little or no per-step human confirmation — that is explicitly not what was built here.

Not this — agentic AI

AI is given a goal and decides the steps, with the human reviewing only the end result, if at all.

This — human-in-the-loop AI

A set of custom Microsoft Copilot assistants, each with one role and working from the product's own requirements and documented principles. One developer reviews and decides before anything reaches the live app.
Built with Microsoft Copilot, not this repository's RDLC OS skill set
This is a separate, real-world instance of the same method — not run through the rdlc-* skills documented elsewhere in this portfolio. The same philosophy (one developer, a role-specific AI assistant per traditional team role, human confirmation before anything ships) was independently built on a different AI platform. That the pattern holds on two different tools, not just one, is itself part of the case for it being a transferable method rather than a feature of one piece of software.

Build from the bottom up: AI only works when the layers below it are in place. Secure: access by role and centre.

Because the requirements came first, they decided the technology — not the other way around. The standardized data needed is small (about 80KB per sync), so the team built on Microsoft 365 tools BC SPCA already licensed, instead of buying a new platform. The live app adds no new software or IT running cost.

4. How AI scaled the implementation

Delivering a data product like this normally takes a team: a project manager, a business analyst, a data analyst, a UX designer, a developer. Here, it was one developer, using a custom Microsoft Copilot assistant for each role.

Custom assistants built in Microsoft Copilot, which BC SPCA already had — no new software cost. And these assistants aren't specific to Live Capacity; they're reused on the next data projects.

Worked example: sketch to live floor plan

Animal Flow sketches a centre's layout by hand and sends it over. By hand, turning that into a working floor plan — resizing the image, then working out the exact x/y position of every kennel button for Power Apps — took at least 4 hours. With the floor plan assistant, under 30 minutes. The developer still checks the result before it goes live. AI assists. People decide.

Before any technology was built, the team wrote down exactly three things — and left the rest out on purpose, pending trusted history.

Already realized: cleaner communication. Animal Flow now only contacts centres the app shows haven't updated, instead of calling every centre for every placement.

Stated as targets, not yet formally measured — kept exactly as honest as the product team itself presented them: placement under 24 hours, and missing kennels down 95%. One real example was observed at a live centre — a placement that would otherwise have taken almost 2 days of calls and emails took under 1 hour on the app — but the team is explicit that this is one anecdotal example, "not yet a formal measure."

Still open, deliberately: capacity forecasting, automated routing, and historical trend analysis all wait on the daily trusted history the product is now building. Rollout is 9 of 31 centres so far, with the remaining 22 the stated next-year target.

8. Why this matters, and what it actually encodes

  • Speed. One developer, with a Copilot assistant standing in for each traditional team role, took a dashboard request to 9 live centres in seven months — work that would normally need a project manager, business analyst, data analyst, UX designer, and developer in sequence.
  • Governance. Live Capacity is explicitly run as a managed data product, not a one-off app — an owner on both the data side and the business side, by design, from before the first line of the build.
  • Repeatability, already claimed by its own authors. The same model is stated to be behind BC SPCA's Foster Analytics work too — this is not being proposed as repeatable here for the first time; it already is, in a second product.
What this process actually encodes
In the product team's own closing words to RSPCA Victoria: "After many years in data and BI, my conclusion is that no product solves this out of the box, because every organization's rules and data are different. Organizations that succeed have someone who owns the connection between operations, data and technology, translating what the intake team needs into rules, data and a working tool." That translation role — not the app itself — is the thing being encoded and reused here.

9. Getting started — adding a centre, or starting your own

Adding a new centre to Live Capacity is five steps, no code:

  1. Sketch the floor plan with the centre manager; AI draws the layout. Owner: Animal Flow + data team
  2. Define the spaces with standard names. Owner: Animal Flow
  3. Set up the same rooms and kennels in ShelterBuddy. Owner: ShelterBuddy admin
  4. Give the centre manager access. Owner: permission list
  5. Centre is live — daily confirmation begins.

And for anyone starting a comparable initiative elsewhere, the team's own three lessons, learned the hard way:

  1. Measure your "before" now. Know how the process works today and what you expect to change, before building anything, so the change can actually be proven later.
  2. Make data consistent first. Standard data and agreed business rules. Nothing works on data nobody can read.
  3. Start small, then grow. Begin with one centre, build a repeatable process, and let real use shape the next features.
If you remember one thing
Start with what your operation needs to react to, and who owns it. Not with the dashboard.
